Malaria - Complete Clinical Notes (Kenya Guidelines 2023)

Writer: Rita - Clinical Medicine Chuka | Reviewed: Based on Kenya National Malaria Guidelines

1. Definition & Epidemiology in Kenya

Protozoal disease caused by Plasmodium spp. transmitted by bite of infected female Anopheles mosquito. Endemic in Nyanza, Western, Coast. P. falciparum causes 98% of cases in Kenya and all severe cases.

2. Etiology

SpeciesFeatureRelapse?
P. falciparumMost severe, high parasitemia, cerebral malariaNo
P. vivax & P. ovaleMilder, dormant hypnozoites in liverYes
P. malariaeChronic, nephrotic syndromeNo

3. Life Cycle (For OSCE)

Mosquito → Sporozoites → Liver (schizonts) → Merozoites → RBCs → Trophozoite → Schizont → Rupture → Fever cycle. In P. vivax/ovale, some become hypnozoites sleeping in liver causing relapse months later.

Why fever is intermittent? RBC rupture releases toxins → cytokines → hypothalamus raises temp. Falciparum rupture is irregular = daily fever. Vivax/ovale = every 48hrs (tertian), Malariae = 72hrs (quartan).

4. Pathophysiology

  • Infected RBCs stick to endothelium (cytoadherence) → blocks capillaries in brain, placenta, kidney = cerebral malaria, AKI
  • Hemolysis → anemia, jaundice, dark urine (blackwater fever)
  • Cytokine storm → fever, rigors, hypoglycemia

5. Clinical Features

Uncomplicated: Fever/chills/rigors (classic paroxysm: cold-hot-sweating), headache, joint pains, vomiting, malaise. No organ dysfunction.

Severe Malaria (WHO Criteria) - Any ONE = Severe:
  • Cerebral: Altered consciousness, seizures, coma (GCS <11)
  • Severe anemia: Hb <5 g/dl
  • AKI: Creatinine >265 umol/L or oliguria
  • Jaundice + parasitemia, Hypoglycemia <2.2 mmol/L
  • Acidosis, Pulmonary edema, Shock, Bleeding/DIC, Hb in urine
  • Hyperparasitemia >10%

6. Investigations

  • Gold standard: Thick & thin blood film - Thick for detection, Thin for species
  • RDT - HRP2 for falciparum (can stay positive 2 weeks after treatment)
  • Hb, Platelets (low), Bilirubin (high), Creatinine, Glucose, LFTs
  • In pregnancy: Always test any fever in endemic area

7. Differential Diagnosis

Typhoid, Dengue, Brucellosis, Sepsis, Meningitis, UTI - All cause fever. Always do malaria test, don't treat blindly.

8. Management - Kenya Guidelines

Uncomplicated Malaria (No danger signs):
First line: Artemether-Lumefantrine (AL / Coartem)
Dose: 4 tablets twice daily x 3 days (adult >35kg). Take with fatty meal/milk.
Weight bands: 5-14kg=1 tab, 15-24kg=2 tabs, 25-34kg=3 tabs

Second line if AL fails: Dihydroartemisinin-Piperaquine (DHA-PPQ)

In pregnancy 1st trimester: Quinine + Clindamycin. 2nd/3rd trimester: AL is safe.
P. vivax/ovale: Add Primaquine 0.25mg/kg daily x 14 days (check G6PD first!)

Severe Malaria = EMERGENCY - Refer + Start:
1. IV Artesunate 2.4mg/kg at 0hr, 12hr, 24hr, then daily. (3mg/kg if <20kg)
2. When can swallow, complete with full AL dose
3. If no Artesunate: IM Artemether or IV Quinine
4. Supportive: Check glucose 4-hourly, manage fever, transfusion if Hb<5, fluids carefully (risk pulmonary edema)
NEVER give IM injection or NSAID in severe malaria

9. Complications

Cerebral malaria, Severe anemia, AKI, Pulmonary edema, Hypoglycemia, Death if untreated

10. Prevention

ITN (LLIN), IRS, IPTp in pregnancy (SP), Chemoprophylaxis for travelers, Environmental - drain stagnant water

🔥 OSCE / Viva - Lecturers Love These

Q1: Why AL is given with fatty meal?
A: Lumefantrine absorption increases 16x with fat.

Q2: Why not give Primaquine without G6PD test?
A: Causes severe hemolysis in G6PD deficient.

Q3: Patient with malaria Hb 4g/dl, what next?
A: Transfuse, but also investigate cause of severe anemia - it's severe malaria.

Past Paper 2024 Chuka: "Discuss management of 2yr old with convulsions and positive RDT" - Answer = Severe malaria with cerebral features → IV Artesunate + manage seizure with diazepam + check glucose.
